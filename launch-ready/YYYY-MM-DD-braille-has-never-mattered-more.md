This series has been about the difference between independence — doing a task alone — and agency, which is controlling the task and its outcome. Part two was about how AI delivers plenty of the first and surprisingly little of the second. This last piece is about the technology that does the opposite.

There is a growing claim among blind and low-vision people that learning braille is becoming pointless. Screen readers got better, the argument goes, AI describes everything now, computer vision reads the world for you. Why spend years learning a tactile code?

I think this gets it exactly backwards. Braille has never mattered more, and the reason is agency.

## The elevator test

AI and computer vision can absolutely deliver independence, as I defined it. What they cannot deliver is control, because you are always waiting on the output of somebody else's system and trusting that it works, that it is online, and that it happens to be right this time.

If you want a concrete example, stand in an elevator and say, "hey Meta, point me to the button for the first floor." After you work out which floor you actually reached, you will appreciate that the braille on the panel would have gotten you to your appointment on time. Or try to figure out which door is the men's room and which is the women's. The words are short and similar, and the signage rarely gives a vision algorithm enough to call it with confidence. Walking into the wrong one, I can attest, is an awkward thing to explain.

My point is not that the algorithms are bad. Their limits have been documented by people much smarter than me. My point is that both of these problems were solved decades ago by six dots on a panel, no battery required. The braille was already there. The only question was whether I could read it.

This is not only about convenience, either. The link between braille and life outcomes is one of the better-documented findings in blindness research: in one widely cited study, 56% of congenitally blind adults who had learned to read with braille were employed, against 23% of those who had learned with print (Ryles, 1996). Correlation is not causation, and the study has its critics. But those numbers are hard to wave away.

## Feeling my own data

Now to the part that excites me most as a researcher: multiline tactile displays. Devices like the Monarch, the Dot Pad, and Graphiti can render braille and tactile graphics on the same refreshable surface (American Foundation for the Blind, 2023). For my money this is the biggest step forward in assistive technology in years — bigger, for working scientists at least, than the smart glasses — because unlike AI description, it hands over full agency.

Think about what changes. If I can feel my own graph, I do not need anyone's summary of it. I judge for myself which part needs work. If I can feel a loss function while a model trains, I can tell it is overfitting and stop it — me, in the moment, not a description after the fact. And the collaboration flips direction: instead of asking sighted colleagues to describe things to me, I can send them my figures, and they receive data from me, the way colleagues do.

After ten years of workarounds, that last part is the one I keep coming back to. With my own hands on my own results, I can finally talk with the people around me about the science — not only about access.
