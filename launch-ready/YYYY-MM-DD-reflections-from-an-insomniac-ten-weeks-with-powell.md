It is fitting that the first entry in my "Reflections from an Insomniac" series comes exactly at the ten-week mark since I got my guide dog, Powell.

Getting a guide dog was the most intense decision I have ever made — and that includes moving abroad from the comfort of Brazil at 18, living in a college dorm in Minneapolis, a study-abroad program in London, and several other less-than-responsible decisions I will probably write about in the coming weeks.

I decided it was time to apply for guide-dog school while I was in Pittsburgh doing my master's at Carnegie Mellon. I honestly cannot recall the precise moment I chose to get a dog, but the thought had been on my mind since I first moved to the United States. The reasons were several: dealing with loneliness, having a companion at home, more freedom in how I move through the world, and the simple fact that I wanted to know what life as a handler was like.

Choosing where to apply was the hardest part. After talking with people who had gone through the process and researching each school, I decided on Guide Dogs for the Blind. The main reason was the infrastructure GDB provides for California residents — by the time I applied, I already knew I was headed to UCSB, so that mattered.

The application process itself was easier than I expected, and there are plenty of resources that explain the mechanics. What I was not ready for was how surreal the whole thing would feel. It never seemed quite real until December 18, 2024, when I got a call during my last final: I was accepted, and classes would start on January 27, 2025. Learning I would train at the Oregon campus instead of San Rafael threw me at first — after a year and a half on the waiting list, I did not know how to feel about it. Some research reassured me that the programs are essentially the same, and besides, I would add one more state to my list.

I am not going to write about the training here; that will probably become its own series of posts. It was very difficult, and it forced me to face things about myself I had comfortably avoided. As an accomplished blind adult, you can start to believe that nothing can beat you — that whatever life throws at you will be easier than what came before. That was decidedly not the case during training. I crashed on the second day, convinced I would never be as independent with my dog as I had imagined. In some ways, that is even true.

I met Powell on January 29 at 2:07 p.m. The video is saved in my iPhone's camera roll as one of the most important moments of my life, and I am so glad it was captured.

On February 9 we flew from Portland to Santa Barbara. As a welcome gift, Powell had a potty accident in the middle of the food court at PDX. He had refused to use the dog-relieving area, and we were both scared, stressed, and exhausted after two weeks of training. In that precise moment I learned two things. First, we shared an emotional connection unlike any I had ever had. Second, challenges would keep coming, and I would need to solve them myself. There was also a third lesson: a complete stranger helped me collect Powell's poop and clean up in my moment of panic. Maybe I would not be that alone after all.

We landed in Santa Barbara and went home to watch the Super Bowl — but we were both so tired that no game was watched.

The first weeks were emotionally hard: adapting to having him at home, figuring out how to handle other people, giving him freedom in the lab, and making my own calls about him — calls that sometimes differed from what I had been taught was correct training, but were the best I could do in the moment. The money I spent was immense, but hearing his tail wag after he jumped onto his new bed made every cent negligible.

After about a month, we flew to Brazil together. Another punch in the face: how do you put a dog on a plane for nineteen hours? How would we fit in the seat? Would he be comfortable? Would I?

It turns out, again, that I was not alone. The flight attendants were kind and helpful, and Powell slept in the aisle — probably better than I did. He refused the relieving areas again, and I panicked mid-journey and called GDB to ask what in the world I should do. They told me to take him outside the airport and let him use the sidewalk, which made me more nervous still, since there was no way I could find someone to walk me back through customs. He held it the entire way to Brazil. He did not complain, and he slept the whole flight.

Brazil was a blast for him: grass to play in, a pool, birds to almost chase. For me it was mental relief. I could sleep again. On the way back I did not even try the relieving areas, and we were fine. Another lesson learned.

A couple of weeks after we returned, we were walking from a lecture back to the lab when he stopped and produced a truly fantastic bout of diarrhea. It continued through the night, and in the morning I took him to the vet. A few days, some medication, and a new food later, he was fine.

To summarize these ten weeks: we flew PDX to SBA, SBA to MCO, MCO to GRU, GRU to ATL, ATL to LAX. We spent countless hours in traffic in both Los Angeles and São Paulo. We had one emergency vet visit. I learned how to give him medication, how to travel with him, and how to navigate my apartment complex with him. It feels like Powell has been in my life for ten years, not ten weeks.

Waking up at night and hearing him settle on his bed. Having him walk up to me in the morning to ask for breakfast. Brushing him. The dog park. These and a hundred other moments have changed my life in ways I cannot fully explain. Ubers are now a problem, so in one narrow sense my mobility has decreased. But holding the handle and letting him guide me produces a feeling for which "mobility" is not even the right word. It is independence, more than anything else I have ever done.

I know there will be more diarrhea, more frustrations, more fears. But the one certainty is that we will be there for each other, and I value that tremendously. I will gladly pick up his poop. And when a migraine leaves me useless on the couch, he puts his head on my leg, and holding him, the migraine almost always fades. So I am no longer certain who is taking care of whom. I am coming to the conclusion that we are taking care of each other in equal parts — and that is exactly how it should be.
