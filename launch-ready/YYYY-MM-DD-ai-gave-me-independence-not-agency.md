In the first piece of this series I argued that independence — doing a task by yourself — is not the same as agency, which is controlling the task and its result. Nowhere is that gap wider than with AI.

Let me start by giving credit. Language models and computer vision have handed blind people a genuinely new kind of independence. I can point a camera at a menu, a whiteboard, a broken appliance, and get an answer in seconds, alone, with no favors owed. Anyone who tells you this is not a big deal has not lived without it.

Now let me tell you what it feels like from the other side of the interaction.

## Someone else decides what the model will do

Unless you have managed to avoid technology news entirely, in which case I envy you, you have seen the arguments about AI companies and governments. The clearest recent case happens to involve the model many of us use daily. Anthropic released Claude Fable 5 and Claude Mythos 5 on June 9, 2026. Three days later, a United States export-control directive forced the company to shut down global access to both models, citing national security after researchers claimed a jailbreak — a characterization Anthropic disputed (Anthropic, 2026; Forbes, 2026). Access came back at the end of June (CNBC, 2026). Three weeks. If you are sighted, that was a news story. If you had built your daily workflow on that model, a tool you rely on simply ceased to exist, by the decision of people you will never meet.

And it is not only governments. The companies decide what their systems will and will not do, and blind users live inside those decisions. We have models that can fabricate a convincing video of a person from a handful of photos, and the same industry will not describe a real photo of a real person standing in front of me. Ask Meta's smart glasses — probably the most useful piece of assistive technology released in the past decade — "hey Meta, describe the person in front of me," and it declines. Be My Eyes had to blur faces in its AI feature for the same policy reasons (Be My Eyes, 2023). I understand the privacy argument. I also notice that nobody asked me, and that a sighted person gets this information for free every time they open their eyes.

## The hallucination problem is not the same problem for me

That models hallucinate is not in dispute. For a sighted reader, a wrong description is usually an annoyance. For a blind researcher it is a different animal.

As I wrote in part one, I have to produce figures to publish, and I use model descriptions to check my own figures. If the model invents detail in my graph, I might submit a paper with a wrong figure in it, or stand at a podium presenting slides that do not show what I am saying they show. My alternatives are not great either. If I ask a sighted colleague to check every figure, I start feeling obligated to put them on the paper. If I hire an assistant, I need one who understands the research well enough to describe a raster plot accurately, and those do not grow on trees.

So this is where AI leaves me: more independent than I have ever been, and yet, at the moments that matter most professionally, still taking somebody's word — a model's word — for what is in front of me, with no way to check it and no say in what it refuses to see.

That would be a bleak place to end the series. It is not the end. In the third piece I want to talk about the technology that does the opposite — that hands control back — and why I think braille, of all things, has never mattered more.
